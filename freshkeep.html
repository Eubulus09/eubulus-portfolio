Case study · Windows desktop · 2026

FreshKeep

A small Windows app that tracks the food in your home, shows what expires next, and turns what gets thrown away into a report you can actually learn from.

Role
Design & development
Platform
Windows 10 / 11
Stack
C++17, Win32 API
Status
Shipped & in use

01 / The problem

Food gets wasted quietly.

Most households do not set out to waste food. It happens because expiry dates live on packaging, in the fridge, and in your head — and none of those places talk to each other. You buy a second bottle of milk, forget the leftovers, and find them a week too late.

Spreadsheets can track this, but they are easy to abandon. Notes apps do not understand dates or quantities. The gap is a tool that opens fast, remembers what you have, and puts the urgent items at the top.

02 / The approach

Local, fast, and quiet.

FreshKeep is a native Windows app written in C++17 against the Win32 API. There is no Electron shell, no .NET runtime, and no web view — the executable talks to Windows directly. It starts instantly and ships without an installer or a runtime dependency.

Everything is stored locally in %APPDATA%\FreshKeep. There is no account, no server, and no telemetry. For people who want more protection, the app offers transparent Windows-account encryption (DPAPI) or a passcode vault using AES-256-GCM.

03 / What it does

The useful details.

  • Expiry-aware list — expired items turn red, items expiring within three days turn amber.
  • Use first — a single view of the items that should be eaten next.
  • Quick add — type a name and the app suggests a category, unit, and shelf life.
  • Barcode friendly — a USB scanner can type straight into the form and remembered barcodes fill in details.
  • Bulk add — enter a whole shopping trip, one item per line.
  • Waste report — money saved and lost, waste by category, and the most-wasted items.
  • Daily checkouts — a per-day log of everything marked used or wasted.
  • Import & export — plain CSV in and out, for spreadsheets and backups.
  • Reminders — a tray icon with a daily nudge about what expires tomorrow.
  • Backups & undo — timestamped backups before saves and Ctrl+Z for the last action.

04 / Technical notes

Constraints as features.

The interface is built from standard Win32 controls with custom row drawing for the inventory list. The whole program is compiled with -mwindows -static-libgcc -static-libstdc++, so a single executable can be copied to another Windows machine and run.

Data is written to a temporary file first and then replaces the old file, which avoids half-written saves. On import, rows with a missing name, an invalid date, a non-positive quantity, or an out-of-range price are skipped instead of corrupting the inventory.

Encryption is handled through DPAPI or a passcode vault using AES-256-GCM and PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 with 600,000 iterations. The recovery key is shown once; the app cannot recover it for you, and it says so plainly.

05 / What I learned

Building FreshKeep taught me how much product thinking happens outside the UI. The hardest parts were not drawing a window — they were deciding what to do when a save fails, how to make undo predictable, and how to explain encryption honestly.

The result is a small tool that does one job well, starts instantly, and keeps a user's data on their own machine. That restraint is the feature.
